# PyroSync — five-minute live pitch

Seven slides. Stop at 5:00, then allow five minutes for judge questions. The 30-second video is an alternative submission demo, not the live pitch.

## Slide 1 · 00:00–00:35

Imagine you are watching fire activity in your country. One satellite records hundreds of observations. Another records thousands. Has fire activity changed, or has the instrument changed? Before we make a decision, we need to understand what those numbers mean. We are Team Gmora, and this is PyroSync. We help people compare satellite fire observations, inspect the evidence, and understand what the data can actually support.

## Slide 2 · 00:35–01:10

Here is the problem in our own data. For Sri Lanka in twenty twenty-five, MODIS recorded six hundred and eighty-four observations. VIIRS on Suomi NPP recorded five thousand, five hundred and nineteen. That is about eight times as many observations. But it does not mean eight times as many separate fires. Instruments see different levels of detail and observe at different times. A useful comparison needs a clear counting rule.

## Slide 3 · 01:10–01:45

PyroSync divides the land into small grid cells. If a cell has at least one vegetation detection that passes our checks on a given day, we count that cell once for that day. We call this an active cell-day. Several observations can contribute, and we keep their source details. This does not reconstruct separate fire incidents. It gives us a shared, repeatable unit, while keeping the evidence attached.

## Slide 4 · 01:45–02:30

This is our working product. Choose Sri Lanka and August. The calendar shows recorded activity through the year. Open the period brief for a plain-language summary. Then open the map and select an area. The inspector explains which sources contributed, with timestamps and original row references. Download the brief, the daily data, or the map data. The same workflow works for Argentina. An analyst can investigate a surprising number and explain where it came from.

## Slide 5 · 02:30–03:25

We test our results instead of trusting a convincing chart. Remove one satellite source, and the system shows how much recorded activity remains. Change the grid or the confidence filter, and the total changes visibly. We also test seven-day forecasts on later years. In Argentina, our chosen model reduced average error by nineteen point two percent in twenty twenty-three and thirty-one point nine percent in twenty twenty-four, compared with repeating last week. Sri Lanka's gains remain inconclusive. Both original twenty twenty-five model choices failed our promotion checks. These are research results, not validated emergency warnings.

## Slide 6 · 03:25–04:15

Our intended users are fire analysts, researchers, and teams preparing environmental reports. Their practical task is to compare, investigate, explain, and share. Our current prototype covers two countries, supported by almost three million historical source records. We have also explored AI-assisted communication. The first study could not support a fair policy comparison. The latest version now checks actor identities before a simulation begins, but the new study and independent review remain pending. Next, we need real-user testing and better information about where satellites had a clear chance to observe.

## Slide 7 · 04:15–05:00

NASA data is the foundation of this work. Our results can be traced from source observations through the counting method and into the evidence brief. Our ask is to validate PyroSync with analysts and researchers, starting with useful, clearly defined tasks. We want to make satellite fire records easier to understand as instruments change. Because the people making important decisions deserve information they can question and explain. We are Team Gmora. PyroSync. Clearer evidence. Stronger decisions. Thank you.
